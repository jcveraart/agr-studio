# Training rationale and evidence

AGR Studio helps an adult recreational rider organize preparation for the Amstel Gold Race Toerversie. It uses the selected route, event date, recent weekly training, available time and days, other sports, training feedback and daily check-ins. It does **not** predict finish time or certify fitness for an event.

The local plan is a transparent rules engine. Its particular progression, scheduling and readiness thresholds have **not been validated in a study**. An optional AI coach can explain the plan and suggest changes; research support for a training principle does not validate a language model or an individual prescription.

## How the planner translates evidence into a usable week

| Principle | App behavior | What remains a coaching choice |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Most endurance work is easy, with a smaller dose of harder work | Conversational endurance and long rides; normally at most one quality cycling session per week. Experienced riders with a performance goal and at least eight available hours may receive two, including hard other sports in that allowance. Hard cycling days are separated by an easy or rest day. | Exact allocation, FTP ranges and 48-hour calendar separation are practical defaults. A fixed 80/20 split is not a universal optimum. |
| Load should reflect current habits | Begin from the lower of recent weekly training and available weekly time. The underlying target grows by about 6% per build week until the time cap; every fourth week is lighter. Someone with no current training starts with at most 90 minutes of easy activity. | The 6% ramp and four-week pattern are conservative product heuristics, not a proven injury prevention formula. Recovery weeks are compared with normal build weeks rather than used as a new low baseline. |
| Reduce fatigue before the event | Reduce the target during the final one or two calendar weeks, retain short controlled work when appropriate, and remove the long ride in the last seven days. | A weekly calendar cannot precisely reproduce every research taper. Individual history, event length and response affect the best duration and reduction. |
| Other activities cost time and recovery | Running, swimming, gym and other activities share the total weekly hours. Hard activities reduce the allowance for hard cycling. Overbooked activities are shortened visibly to fit the target. A busy-week cap can reduce that week's hours, including all sports. Actual logged minutes and manual activities consume the current week's remaining allowance. | Equal minutes or equal RPE do not mean equal physiological or musculoskeletal stress across sports. A hard run may need more recovery than a bike ride. A busy-week cap is availability, not a recovery diagnosis. |
| Subjective feedback is useful | Fatigue, sleep, soreness, stress, illness, pain and unexpectedly high RPE trigger fewer minutes and easy sessions for the coming days. Past and completed workouts stay intact. | These short custom questions and thresholds are not validated wellness instruments. A low readiness score is not an injury prediction or diagnosis. |
| A missed session should not create a training pile-up | Never add intensity as compensation for a missed session. Continue with the next suitable workout. | A prolonged break needs a revised recent training input and a new gradual progression. |
| Fueling is a skill to practice | Long-ride descriptions encourage food and drink rehearsal. The event section can offer starting carbohydrate ranges by duration. | Gut tolerance, weather, access to drinks and individual needs matter. Do not assume every rider should jump to a high intake. |

The **weekly hours setting includes all planned sports**, while the current-hours input represents recent total training. Event riding time is an illustrative estimate based on distance and broad experience level, is explicitly not a predicted result, and is excluded from the training budget. Power zones are estimates based on the FTP supplied by the rider; the talk test and perceived effort matter, and heart rate is not used to invent individual thresholds.

## Evidence and limitations

**Intensity distribution.** Stöggl and Sperlich randomly assigned 48 trained endurance athletes from several sports to different nine-week training distributions. The polarized group improved several endurance outcomes more than the comparison groups in that experiment. This supports combining substantial easy training with limited harder work; the population and short study do not establish one ideal distribution for every amateur cyclist. [Original study, Frontiers in Physiology (2014)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24550842/).

**Taper.** In a small study of trained cyclists, Neary and colleagues compared seven-day tapers with different reductions in training volume while retaining substantial intensity. It informs the idea of reducing fatigue before an event. Small samples and laboratory time trials limit transfer to a recreational hill ride. [Original study, Dynamic Medicine (2005)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15790400/). A later randomized study of 24 high-level male cyclists compared a reduced-volume taper with continued progressive training. [Original study, Journal of Sports Science and Medicine (2013)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24149602/). For wider context, taper research favors lowering volume while preserving an appropriate amount of intensity; exact reduction and duration vary. [Mujika and Padilla review (2003)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12840640/).

**Training load and session RPE.** Foster and colleagues studied cycle exercise and basketball and found session-RPE load related consistently to heart-rate load. The app displays minutes × session RPE as an understandable record of perceived load. It is an arbitrary-unit measure; it is not TSS, a direct fitness measurement or an injury risk threshold. [Original comparative study, Journal of Strength and Conditioning Research (2001)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11708692/).

**Recovery feedback.** Athlete monitoring research supports tracking subjective experience. Saw and colleagues reviewed 56 original studies and found self-reported measures often responded to training changes more consistently than common objective measurements. Those results support listening to riders, but do not validate this app’s custom five-point questions or its cutoffs. [Research review, British Journal of Sports Medicine (2016)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4789708/).

**Strength and concurrent exercise.** Rønnestad and colleagues studied 20 well-trained cyclists over 12 weeks, comparing cycling with and without added lower-body strength training. The combined group improved selected strength and cycling outcomes. This supports strength as a possible complement, not a claim that any gym session produces the same benefit. Their supervised heavy-strength protocol should not be inferred from the app’s generic “strength and control” library item. [Original study, European Journal of Applied Physiology (2010)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19960350/). A related small study examined maintaining strength during the competition season. [Original study (2010)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20799042/).

**Carbohydrate during prolonged cycling.** In randomized exercise trials with eight trained cyclists, Jentjens and Jeukendrup found combined glucose and fructose could increase oxidation of ingested carbohydrate compared with a single source at high intake. This mechanistic result supports using mixed carbohydrate sources when practicing higher hourly intakes, but is not an individual tolerance test. [Original study, Journal of Applied Physiology (2004)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14657042/). A practical starting range commonly used for rides of roughly one to two and a half hours is **30–60 g per hour**; longer rides can use **60–90 g per hour** when practiced and tolerated, with multiple carbohydrate sources at the higher end. These are starting ranges, not an obligation or a maximum applicable to every athlete. [Jeukendrup evidence review, Sports Medicine (2014)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24791914/).

## What this version deliberately does not infer

The planner has no measured lactate thresholds, power-duration curve, validated recovery model or knowledge of medical history. It does not diagnose illness, prescribe rehabilitation, predict overtraining, use acute:chronic workload ratios as injury risk, or claim that increasing volume by a fixed percentage is universally safe. A recorded illness or pain pauses upcoming suggestions; the rider can review and update the plan rather than being automatically declared recovered after a timer.

Research reviewed on **11 October 2026**. The event information has its own organizer sources in the app; route and date changes should be checked against those sources.
